Once a test is live, Kommerce reads it every day and tells you what each idea deserves.

## What you do each day

<Steps>
  <Step title="Read the day's calls">
    Each idea gets a recommendation and the numbers behind it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Approve, or don't">
    Nothing moves budget until applied.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Step in where you know more">
    A stock problem or a client decision is invisible to the engine.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## What the recommendations mean

These are the exact phrases you will see.

| What it says | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Keep running** | Too early, or going fine |
| **Add funding** | Close. Deserves more room |
| **Passed: confirming on fresh spend** | Cleared the first bar. Proving it again |
| **Back to testing** | Did not hold up on second look |
| **Winner: move it to scale** | Proven twice |
| **Pause early** | Losing badly enough to stop now |
| **Kill** | Clearly not working |
| **Stop: out of funding, no verdict** | Spent its budget, showed nothing |
| **Stop: promising, unproven** | Ran out of money while still looking good |

<Warning>
  "Promising, unproven" is not a failure. The idea looked like it was working and ran out of budget before it could prove it. These are your best retest candidates. Treating them as kills throws away the ideas most likely to win next time.
</Warning>

## Why an idea has to win twice

An idea that passes its first run does not go straight to scale. It gets a second, separate run on fresh spend.

A handful of early sales can make a mediocre idea look excellent. Something that got lucky passes the first round and fails the second. It goes back to testing instead of to scale.

By the time you see "Winner: move it to scale", the idea has been right twice on different money.

## Reading the numbers

The number that decides is cost per order against break-even CPA. Everything else is diagnosis.

A single cost figure says an idea failed. The funnel says where.

| Pattern | Points at |
| --- | --- |
| Few impressions | Budget, bid or audience. Not creative |
| Impressions, no clicks | The hook. First line or first frame |
| Clicks, no add-to-cart | The ad-to-page match, or the offer |
| Add-to-cart, no checkout | Price, shipping or trust |
| Checkout, no order | Payment, or something broken |

Only the first two are creative problems.

Ideas are judged against what is typical for this account over recent weeks, not against an industry benchmark.

### Numbers that mislead

**CTR without orders.** Cheap clicks that do not convert cost money and teach nothing.

**ROAS on tiny samples.** Four orders at a great return is noise. Under about ten orders, treat anything as directional.

**Anything inside 72 hours.** Meta is still learning.

## How the signals work

Kommerce does not say "this ad is good". It says how likely an idea is to beat break-even given what it has seen.

A simple rule like "kill anything above three times break-even" ignores how much evidence there is. Two orders and two hundred orders get treated the same, and that is how good ideas die young.

Working in probabilities means the number carries its own confidence. An idea at 55% after heavy spend is genuinely mediocre. The same 55% after two orders means we do not know yet.

| Reading | Means |
| --- | --- |
| 90% likely to beat break-even | Passes its first run |
| Holds up on fresh spend | Becomes a winner |
| Drops below a floor while confirming | Back to testing |
| Still around 50% after its funding | Promising, unproven |

This is why the engine will keep funding something that looks unremarkable, and stop something that looks fine. It reads the strength of the evidence, not just the average.

## The account brake

Kommerce watches the whole account, not just your test. It pulls back when CPMs run at twice the account's normal level, or account-wide cost per order sits above one and a half times break-even for three days.

If the brake comes on, the account is in trouble, not your test. Find out what changed at account level first.
