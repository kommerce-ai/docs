---
title: "When to kill, iterate or graduate"
description: "The three outcomes, what each one means, and the one people get wrong."
---

Every idea ends in one of three places. Telling them apart correctly is most of the skill in running tests.

## The three outcomes

| Outcome | Means | What you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Kill** | Clearly not working, with enough evidence to say so | Stop. Don't retest without a reason |
| **Iterate** | Something here worked | Make another version of the part that worked |
| **Graduate** | Proven twice | Move it to [scaling](/operator-wiki/performance/scaling) |

## The one people get wrong

**"Stop: promising, unproven" is not a kill.**

It means the idea ran out of funding while still looking plausible. The evidence is incomplete, not negative.

<Warning>
  Treating promising ideas as failures is the most expensive habit in testing. You throw away ideas you've already part-funded, then spend fresh money discovering something similar.

  Keep a list. These are your first candidates when budget frees up.
</Warning>

## When to overrule

The engine reads numbers. You know things it doesn't:

- Stock ran out mid-test, so orders stopped for a reason unrelated to the ad
- A promotion ran, inflating a window
- The landing page broke
- A competitor ran an aggressive offer that week

<Note>
  Overruling is legitimate and expected. What matters is recording *why* — the reason is what makes the result interpretable later, and the learning useful.
</Note>

## What iterate actually means

Not "make it again, differently." Iterate on **the specific thing that worked**.

If the hook earned cheap clicks but the body lost them, keep the hook and change the body. If an angle worked for one persona, try that angle on an adjacent one.

Labels are what make this possible — Kommerce reads each idea's angle, audience and format when suggesting what to iterate on. An unlabelled idea produces vaguer suggestions.

## Closing it out

A test ends as **winner**, **loser** or **inconclusive**, pointing at scale, iterate or stop.

Write the verdict in your own words. The draft knows the numbers; it doesn't know the client conversation or the stock problem that explains them — and that context is the part worth keeping.
