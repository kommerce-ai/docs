## The number that decides

**Cost per order against break-even CPA.** Everything else is diagnosis.

A test doesn't pass by having a good CTR. It passes by producing orders at or under the bar, with enough evidence to believe it.

## Reading down the funnel

A single cost-per-order figure says an idea failed. The funnel says *where*:

| Pattern | What it points at |
| --- | --- |
| Few impressions | Budget, bid or audience — not creative |
| Impressions, no clicks | The hook. First line or first frame |
| Clicks, no add-to-cart | Mismatch between ad and page, or the offer |
| Add-to-cart, no checkout | Price, shipping, or trust |
| Checkout, no order | Payment, or something broken |

<Note>
  Only the first two are creative problems. The rest are funnel problems that more creative won't fix — which is why Kommerce flags **fix the funnel** rather than suggesting more ads.
</Note>

## Judged against the account, not zero

An idea is compared to what's typical for *this* account over recent weeks, not to an industry benchmark. A 1% CTR might be excellent or poor depending on the account.

## Numbers that mislead

**CTR without orders.** Cheap clicks that don't convert cost money and teach nothing. Curiosity-led copy does this reliably.

**ROAS on tiny samples.** Four orders at a great return is noise. Under about ten orders, treat anything as directional.

**Early-window anything.** Inside 72 hours, Meta is still learning.

<Warning>
  Checkout and purchase numbers measure your **tracking** as much as your ads. If the audit flagged signal problems, fix those before you believe any verdict built on them.
</Warning>

## How confident is confident

Kommerce works in probabilities, not thresholds — see [Reading the signals](/operator-wiki/performance/bayesian-signals). An idea passes at **90%** confidence of beating break-even, and that's deliberately a high bar: you're about to put real budget behind it.
