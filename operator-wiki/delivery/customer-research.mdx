Copy written from instinct reads like copy. Copy written from customers' own words reads like recognition. The difference is research.

## Where the words come from

| Source | What you get |
| --- | --- |
| **Product review themes** | The richest source you have. Real phrasing, real objections |
| **Persona pain, objections, motivations** | Structured, but a hypothesis until validated |
| **Copy of winning ads** | What already worked in this account |
| **Competitor wording** | Patterns worth borrowing, and phrasing worth avoiding |
| **Category reviews on the web** | Useful when the brand's own reviews are thin |

## The method

Copy and paste exact phrases — don't paraphrase them. Sort them into four buckets: **pain**, **desired outcome**, **objection**, and **rave**. Star the most vivid.

Starred phrases become headline candidates almost verbatim. That's the point of the exercise.

<Warning>
  Never quote a review as a testimonial on an ad unless it's real, attributable, and the brand can produce it on request.
</Warning>

## Personas

A persona is a hypothesis about who buys and why. Kommerce stores them with their pain, objections and motivations, and every brief is written against exactly one.

<Note>
  A persona taken from a brand questionnaire is **unvalidated** — say so when you cite it. A persona confirmed by review language or by test results is evidence. The difference matters when you're deciding how much budget an idea deserves.
</Note>

Be specific about who actually buys rather than who the brand wishes bought. A persona defined too broadly produces copy that lands with nobody.

## Tagging what you find

Every claim that reaches a brief carries a tag saying where it came from — review language, persona, pattern, learning, competitor, or the brand itself.

A line with no tag doesn't belong in a hypothesis. If an idea has no supporting evidence it can still be worth testing, but label it **exploratory** and keep it to the minimum budget.
