One number decides whether a test passed: **break-even CPA**, the most an order can cost before it stops making money.

## Working it out

Break-even CPA comes from order value and margin. Break-even ROAS is the same fact expressed as a ratio.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **AOV** | Average order value |
| **COGS** | Cost of goods — what fulfilling an order costs |
| **Break-even ROAS** | The return at which you make nothing |
| **Break-even CPA** | The order cost at which you make nothing |

## Where Kommerce gets the number

In order of preference:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Frozen on the test">
    A figure set when the test was created. Most reliable, because it can't drift mid-test.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The brand's unit economics">
    Taken from the brand record.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Estimated from order value and break-even ROAS">
    Used when neither of the above exists — and **marked as assumed**.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
  Check whether a bar is assumed before you trust a verdict built on it. An assumed bar isn't wrong, but it's an estimate, and every kill and graduation inherits its error.
</Warning>

## Why an optimistic bar is expensive

Set the bar lower than true break-even and two things happen at once, both invisible:

- Ideas that were making money get killed for missing a bar they never had to clear
- The engine's confidence calculations are measured against the wrong line, so "passed" means less than it looks

The second is worse, because it produces winners that don't scale — and you won't find out until you've put real budget behind one.

<Tip>
  If a client won't give you real COGS, ask for a range and use the pessimistic end. An overly cautious bar costs you some good ideas. An overly optimistic one costs you the account's credibility.
</Tip>
