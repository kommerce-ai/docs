Creative strategy decides what to say and to whom. Production decides how it looks. Confusing the two produces beautiful ads that test nothing.

## What you decide

<Steps>
  <Step title="Who it is for">
    Exactly one persona. Not a blend.
  </Step>
  <Step title="What they already know">
    The awareness level. This decides what your opening line may say.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The angle">
    The claim or positioning you are arguing.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The one variable">
    What changes between versions. Everything else is held constant.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The mode">
    Static, video, motion or creator. Chosen from evidence and timeline, not taste.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Customer awareness levels

How much the reader already knows decides what your headline is allowed to say.

| Level | The headline's job | Opens on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Unaware** | Make her recognise herself or a moment. No product yet | A scene or an identity |
| **Problem Aware** | Name the pain in her words. Hint there is a way out | The problem |
| **Solution Aware** | Say why this kind of fix beats the others she knows | The mechanism |
| **Product Aware** | Prove this product. Specifics and proof | The product |
| **Most Aware** | Give the deal and a reason to act now | The offer |

An ad opening on a discount speaks to someone already choosing between brands. Show it to someone who does not know she has the problem and it is noise.

The reverse fails too. A long story about recognising a feeling wastes a reader who already has her card out.

### The same product at five levels

For a supplement that helps sleep.

- **Unaware.** "3pm and you're reading the same sentence again."
- **Problem Aware.** "Still tired after eight hours? It isn't the hours."
- **Solution Aware.** "Not melatonin. A magnesium blend that works with your own cycle."
- **Product Aware.** "4,000 reviews. Average 23 minutes faster to sleep."
- **Most Aware.** "30% off the sleep bundle. Ends Sunday."

<Note>
  Each idea carries exactly one awareness label. If the opening words do not match that label, either the copy is wrong or the label is wrong. Fix it at brief stage, where it is nearly free.
</Note>

### Picking a level

Most accounts have plenty of Most Aware ads. Retargeting fills that in by itself. The gap is usually higher up the funnel.

Selecting several awareness levels in one test multiplies your output and your budget. Each level is a different idea and needs its own spend.

## Copy comes from customers

Copy written from instinct reads like copy. Copy written from customers' words reads like recognition.

Pull review themes, persona objections, the copy of winning ads, claimable numbers, and competitor wording. Copy exact phrases rather than paraphrasing. Sort them into pain, desired outcome, objection and rave, and star the vivid ones. Those become headlines almost verbatim.

### Appeals, in order of pull

1. **Self-interest.** What she gets.
2. **News.** What has changed.
3. **Curiosity.** What she does not know.

Curiosity alone is weak. It earns the click and loses the sale, which on a cost-per-order bar is worse than no click.

### Rules that always hold

- Specific beats vague. "23 minutes faster" beats "sleep better".
- Her words beat yours.
- Claims must be claimable. Approved claims verbatim, banned words absent.
- Never fake a testimonial.
- Vary the lever, not the wording. Ads in one ad set should not be paraphrases.

Meta primary text truncates. Aim for 125 characters or fewer. The hard limit is 400.

<Warning>
  In supplements and skincare, a banned claim reaching a live ad is a legal problem, not a style note.
</Warning>

## Long-form is a different craft

A long-form static runs 600 to 2,000 words: pattern-interrupt hook, symptom cascade, named mechanism, ingredient stack, delayed brand introduction, transformation timeline, call to action.
