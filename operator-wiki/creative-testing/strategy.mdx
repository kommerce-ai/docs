---
title: "Creative strategy"
description: "Deciding what to say and to whom, and matching it to what the reader already knows."
---

Creative strategy decides **what to say and to whom**. Production decides how it looks. Confusing the two produces beautiful ads that test nothing.

## What you decide

<Steps>
  <Step title="Who it is for">
    Exactly one persona. Not a blend.
  </Step>
  <Step title="What they already know">
    The awareness level. This decides what your opening line may say.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The angle">
    The claim or positioning you are arguing.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The one variable">
    What changes between versions. Everything else is held constant.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The mode">
    Static, video, motion or creator. Chosen from evidence and timeline, not taste.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Customer awareness levels

This is the most useful idea in direct response. How much the reader already knows decides what your headline is allowed to say.

| Level | The headline's job | Opens on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Unaware** | Make her recognise herself or a moment. No product yet | A scene or an identity |
| **Problem Aware** | Name the pain in her words. Hint there is a way out | The problem |
| **Solution Aware** | Say why this kind of fix beats the others she knows | The mechanism |
| **Product Aware** | Prove this product. Specifics and proof | The product |
| **Most Aware** | Give the deal and a reason to act now | The offer |

An ad opening on a discount speaks to someone already choosing between brands. Show it to someone who does not know she has the problem and it is noise. She has no frame to put the offer in.

The reverse fails too. A long story about recognising a feeling wastes a reader who already has her card out.

<Note>
  Each idea carries exactly one awareness label. If the opening words do not match that label, either the copy is wrong or the label is wrong. Fix it at brief stage, where it is nearly free.
</Note>

### The same product at five levels

For a supplement that helps sleep.

- **Unaware.** "3pm and you're reading the same sentence again."
- **Problem Aware.** "Still tired after eight hours? It isn't the hours."
- **Solution Aware.** "Not melatonin. A magnesium blend that works with your own cycle."
- **Product Aware.** "4,000 reviews. Average 23 minutes faster to sleep."
- **Most Aware.** "30% off the sleep bundle. Ends Sunday."

Only one of those is right for a given audience.

### Picking a level

Most accounts have plenty of Most Aware ads. Retargeting fills that in by itself. The gap is usually higher up the funnel, where you pay to reach people who do not know the brand.

Selecting several awareness levels in one test multiplies your output and your budget. Each level is a different idea and needs its own spend. Testing one properly beats testing three thinly.

## Decide from evidence

Before any creative thinking, read what the account knows. Its learnings, its patterns, past tests, account numbers and competitor research.

Every claim that reaches a brief carries a tag saying where it came from. State how strong it is.

| Strength | Roughly |
| --- | --- |
| Strong | Repeated, with real spend behind it |
| Likely | Good signal, decent sample |
| Leaning | Suggestive |
| Directional | Under about ten orders, or an early pattern |
| Thin | Barely anything |
| Against | Evidence points the other way |

Never contradict a learning silently. If a test argues against something the account established, cite it and say why re-testing is worth the money.

An idea with no supporting evidence can still be worth testing. Label it **exploratory** and keep it to the minimum budget. What is not allowed is an unsupported idea dressed up as an evidenced one.

## Hold the variable constant

The whole value of a test is being able to attribute the result.

Format is held constant inside a test. If the strategy calls for two modes, that is two tests asking the same question.
